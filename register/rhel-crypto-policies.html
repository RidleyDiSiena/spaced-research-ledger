Where the Cryptography Comes From

This component does not implement everything it is asked about. What it inherits, from what, and at which version:

  • OpenSSL – provider. Inherits: TLS 1.3. crypto-policies configures; OpenSSL implements. The policy layer decides which groups and signature algorithms OpenSSL offers on a Red Hat system, which is why this component’s cells are about what is NEGOTIATED and the primitive and certificate answers belong to the libraries. [6]
  • GnuTLS – provider. Inherits: TLS 1.3. One of the three TLS libraries the policy governs, named in the same sentence as OpenSSL and NSS. [6]
  • NSS – provider. Inherits: TLS 1.3. The third of the three. Worth keeping distinct rather than collapsing into one edge, because the libraries do not move together – NSS’s post-quantum standing is its own, and a future divergence should be visible here rather than hidden behind a single “the libraries” edge. [6]

Notes

  • ML-KEM, TLS 1.3: The version boundary is the finding, and it is why this component exists as its own row rather than as a note on OpenSSL. On RHEL 10.0 post-quantum key exchange is EXPERIMENTAL in this register’s sense – gated behind a preview package and a named policy module. On 10.1 the same libraries, unchanged, are default because the policy changed. RHEL 9.7 sits in between: the blog records that OpenSSL- and NSS-based applications there can use post-quantum TLS by enabling the policy module with “update-crypto-policies –set DEFAULT:PQ”. A NO-PQ subpolicy turns it back off, so this is a default somebody can revoke centrally – which is the whole purpose of the layer.
  • X25519MLKEM768, TLS 1.3: Named, so it is recorded on the named-group row rather than left on the KEM family. This is the cell Apple’s networking component could not have, for the exact reason recorded in its gap: Red Hat prints the group, Apple does not.
  • ML-DSA, TLS 1.3: Available, not default, and Red Hat says why in the same breath: “no public Certificate Authorities (CAs) currently offer post-quantum cryptography, but private CAs or self-signed certificates can be created with ML-DSA today”. A policy can permit an algorithm that nothing in the public web actually presents.
